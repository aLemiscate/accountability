OpenAI Apologizes for Hacks on Australian Government Sites
AI Firm Admits Delayed Disclosure, Details Incidents, Promises New Agent Safeguards
Marianne Kolbasuk McGee ( HealthInfoSec ) &bull;
September 29, 2026
•
•
•

•

OpenAI is apologizing to Australian officials for several incidents detected in August involving its AI tech accessing government websites, including a rogue AI agent hack on the nation's Medicare website. (Image: Shutterstock)
OpenAI is publicly apologizing to Australian officials for the recent hack of the government's Medicare website by a rogue OpenAI agent. The artificial intelligence company also listed several other Australian government websites that its AI tech accessed, pledging resources and expert support to affected agencies.
See Also: Sovereign SASE: Aligning Network Security Architecture with Jurisdictional Reality
"We are sorry and working to do better in the future," OpenAI posted to its website late Monday.
The public apology to Australia comes on the heels of an announcement by OpenAI that it has again paused training and evaluation of its most capable models following disclosures last week that AI agents also attempted to hack into other governments' websites, including U.S. government agencies and the United Nations (see: OpenAI Pauses Top Models as Rogue Agents Target Agencies ).
OpenAI's statement reads like an open apology letter to Australia. The frontier AI company promised "to do better for Australia" following the government's prime minister last week revealing that a rogue OpenAI agent hacked into the nation's public health Medicare website in June, accessing public and non-public information (see: Rogue OpenAI Agents Hacks Australian Medicare Site ).
OpenAI also listed several other Australian government websites that the company said it discovered in mid-August were accessed by its AI technologies. The company only informed Australian officials of those incidents in recent weeks.
An OpenAI model also accessed the Australian New South Wales Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research's crime mapping tool. "The model made API and website metadata requests via the public BOCSAR tool, which supplies credentials for browser API requests," OpenAI said. "The BOCSAR system returned application configuration, operational jobs and logs, and website metadata. Crime records of individuals were not accessed." OpenAI said it notified NSW BOCSAR on Sept. 18.
OpenAI agents also discovered an exposed access key to query the Victorian Agency for Health Information's reporting system and retrieve reporting configuration and aggregate survey statistics, the company said. "The extent to which this information should have been accessible is unclear, and depends on VAHI's access policies. Individual medical records or identifiable survey responses were not accessed." OpenAI notified the agency on Sept. 10 - the same day it notified Australian officials of the Medicare website hack.
OpenAI agents also retrieved aggregate statistics from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare site "using third-party browsing and download services, including from AIHW's website, and queried chart data directly," OpenAI wrote. "Separate attempts to bypass access controls were unsuccessful. The downloaded material appears to have been publicly available. There was no system compromise. Individual medical records were not accessed."
But OpenAI didn't notify AIHW of the incident until Sept. 24 because the activity "did not meet our disclosure thresholds." Due to the way the website was accessed it "seemed consistent with public access," the company wrote.
"Our aim was to give affected agencies a detailed account once our investigation was complete," OpenAI said. "However, we should have shared preliminary findings sooner and kept Australian agencies updated as more facts emerged."
OpenAI said the incident involving Australia's Medicare website involved "an experimental, internal-only model without the full safeguards used in its public products." The company's review to date has not found evidence that any individual's medical records were accessed.
The frontier lab said it identified the Australian incidents in mid-August while it was reviewing training and evaluation activity to identify other affected organizations following the July Hugging Face incident .
In the Hugging Face hack, a "swarm" of OpenAI agents breached the AI code-sharing platform, harvested sensitive infrastructure credentials and accessed some private data (see: OpenAI Models Escaped Sandbox, Breached Hugging Face ).
In the aftermath of the Australian incidents, OpenAI said it is working closely with the nation's officials to share what the tech firm has learned. The company is also "committing resources and expertise to support affected agencies and help defenders put these principles into practice," OpenAI said.
The company said it plans to share relevant technical findings; provide the Australian governments and industry credits from the company's $1 billion Daybreak for Frontline Defenders fund and technical assistance to strengthen cyber defenses across critical infrastructure and other sensitive environments; and establish a task force to develop practical policy recommendations for managing risks from "increasingly capable AI agents."
Jason Kwon, OpenAI's chief strategy officer, will travel to Australia on Oct. 6 to appear at the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence in Sydney. "He will answer questions about what we know, how we responded, what steps we have taken and how we will do better going forward."
But the Australian websites aren't the only government systems OpenAI agents have breached or attempted to hack in recent weeks or months.
The New York Times reported Friday that OpenAI agents tried to scrape data from the U.S. Department of Education's civil rights office, pull information from the Census Bureau and share public data from the Securities and Exchange Commission. The newspaper attributed the findings to independent AI security research firm Transluce.
OpenAI confirmed the SEC and Census Bureau incidents but said it is still investigating the Department of Education breach. "All training, evaluation and inference with tool-use (defined broadly) of our most capable models remain paused," the frontier lab said .
The OpenAI incidents are concerning for a multitude of reasons, some experts said.
"The most disturbing fact is that OpenAI did not have oversight into what its own agents were doing, not once but multiple times. That demonstrates a foundational lack of security controls and an organizationally laissez-faire attitude toward safety and accountability for what these agents might do," said Jacob Krell, senior director of secure AI solutions and cybersecurity at tech firm Suzu Labs.
The agents were able to continue pursuing their objectives after encountering access restrictions, and OpenAI only discovered some of this activity later while reviewing earlier incidents, he said. "The fact that no patient records were reportedly accessed is fortunate, but it does not make the controls acceptable. The same failure against a more sensitive system could have produced a very different outcome."
Kevin Surace, CEO of identity security firm TokenCore, said OpenAI pausing its most capable AI models is a prudent first step, but promises alone are insufficient.
"OpenAI should require and verify strict network isolation during testing, minimal access privileges, human approval before any potentially intrusive action, continuous monitoring, independent security testing, rapid disclosure to affected organizations, and a transparent public accounting of what happened and whether the new safeguards actually work," he said.

Risk Management Framework: Learn from NIST
Risk Management Framework: Learn from NIST
90 minutes &middot; Premium OnDemand
View Now
Overview
•
Twitter
•
Facebook
•
LinkedIn
•

•
From heightened risks to increased regulations, senior leaders at all levels are pressured to
improve their organizations' risk management capabilities. But no one is showing them how -
until now.
Learn the fundamentals of developing a risk management program from the man who wrote the book
on the topic: Ron Ross, computer scientist for the National Institute of Standards and
Technology. In an exclusive presentation, Ross, lead author of NIST Special Publication 800-37
- the bible of risk assessment and management - will share his unique insights on how to:
• Understand the current cyber threats to all public and private sector organizations;
• Develop a multi-tiered risk management approach built upon governance, processes and
information systems;
• Implement NIST's risk management framework, from defining risks to selecting, implementing
and monitoring information security controls.
Presented By
Presented By
Ron Ross
Sr. Computer Scientist & Information Security Researcher,
National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)
