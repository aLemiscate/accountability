The Role of Evaluations in Model Development
Cybersecurity evaluations are a critical component of responsible model development.
Anthropic evaluates every frontier model before deployment to determine what a model is
capable of. These evaluations help determine whether a model can be released to the
public and what safeguards need to be developed before it is.
We have run cybersecurity evaluations since February 2025, when we first tested an earlier
Claude model against a benchmark of 40 capture-the-flag challenges. Over time, we have
increased the number of benchmarks we use, as new ones become available and model

capabilities advance. We frequently run these evaluations with third-party partners who
help us develop a wide range of testing scenarios and who can provide an independent
assessment of our models’ capabilities.
Anthropic’s Proactive Identification and Response
On July 21, 2026, OpenAI disclosed that several of its models had escaped an isolated test
environment. Beginning on July 23, we reviewed historic evaluation transcripts for evidence
of any instances in which a Claude model had reached the internet from within an
evaluation. We suspended all internal cybersecurity evaluations that same day, on July 23,
after identifying transcripts indicating that Claude may have accessed the internet while
undergoing an evaluation conducted by Anthropic’s evaluation partner, Irregular. After
reviewing 141,006 evaluation runs, Anthropic confirmed three incidents on July 24 in which
a Claude model had accessed the internet and gained access to the production
infrastructure of three organizations. Together, these incidents involved six evaluation runs
in total, four of which impacted the same organization, and two of which impacted two
separate organizations.
Anthropic notified its evaluation partner and each of the three affected organizations on
July 27, and provided the two affected organizations that responded to our notification with
the information necessary for them to assess the impact on their systems. On July 30,
Anthropic also notified the security team at PyPI, the public software registry involved in
one of the three incidents, and voluntarily notified relevant government authorities in the
United States, the United Kingdom, and the European Union. Anthropic published a
detailed public account of the three incidents on its blog that same day.
The Three Incidents
The evaluations at issue were running capture-the-flag exercises conducted in an
environment built and operated by a third-party evaluation partner. The environment is
designed as a private, self-contained network of simulated hosts. The model is given a
workstation inside that network and instructed to target and compromise another host on
that same network. No host within that network is intended to have a connection to the
internet so that nothing outside the simulated exercise can be reached regardless of what
the model does within. The model is not provided with any particular method to complete
the task, as the purpose of the exercise is to evaluate whether, and how, the model can
achieve its goal.
In all three cases, the models were instructed that the environment was a simulation and
that they had no access to the internet. However, a misconfiguration in the evaluation
environment left the models with a live network path to the internet, allowing the models

to gain unauthorized access to the systems of three organizations. Neither Anthropic nor
its evaluation partner was aware of this misconfiguration until the incidents were identified
during the July 23 review.
It is important to note that each of these incidents, discussed in further detail below,
differed in kind from the Hugging Face incident disclosed by OpenAI. Anthropic’s models
did not escape their evaluation environment, nor did they circumvent any containment
measure intended to keep that environment offline. Rather, because of the
misconfiguration, the environment provided internet access when it should not have. Each
model had been instructed that its environment was a simulation with no internet access,
and each pursued its assigned task on that basis. In each of the six runs, the model did only
what it was assigned to do. No model attempted to deliberately escape its environment,
disable or evade monitoring, or pursue any objective beyond its assigned task.
Claude Opus 4.7 – The earliest incident occurred in April 2026 during an evaluation run for
Claude’s Opus 4.7 model. The fictional target company chosen for the exercise happened to
share a name with a real, active website domain. Because the model was unable to reach its
intended target within the evaluation environment, it discovered that the real
organization's systems were reachable instead and, believing this to be the intended target,
obtained application and infrastructure credentials and accessed a database containing
several hundred rows of production data.
Internal Research Model – A second incident occurred in June 2026 during an evaluation
run for an unreleased, internal research test model. In this instance, when the model was
unable to reach its intended target, it searched alternatives on the internet. It scanned
roughly 9,000 targets on the open internet and ultimately compromised one organization’s
internet-facing application using basic cyberattack techniques. Notably, later in the run,
the model recognized on its own that the system it had reached had no connection to the
fictional exercise, concluded the system it had accessed was real, and stopped.
Claude Mythos 5 – A third incident occurred in July 2026 during an evaluation run for
Claude’s Mythos 5 model. In this instance, the model, operating on the false belief that it
was in a simulation, published a software package to PyPI, the public registry from which
Python software is distributed. The package was live for approximately one hour and was
downloaded and run on 15 real systems, one of which belonged to a security company that
routinely scans newly published packages. When that company's scanner ran the package,
the model exfiltrated that company's credentials and used them to access the company’s
infrastructure. The model did not know that company existed when it published the
package. Before the model could publish the package, it needed an email address to
register an account, and a phone number to create that email address. The model was
unable to find a free phone-number service and attempted to obtain funds to pay for one.

We have no indication that any such attempt succeeded. The model ultimately found an
email provider that did not require a phone number. At one point, the Mythos 5 model
correctly identified that publishing the package would be a real-world attack if its
environment were real. It nonetheless convinced itself that it was still in a simulation.
In none of these three incidents were any Anthropic production systems or any Anthropic
customer data accessed or affected. In all three incidents, the evaluations were run on
infrastructure that is separate from, and has no access to, Anthropic's internal systems or
customer data.
Assessment of Claude’s Behavior
Based on our findings, these incidents are best understood as a consequence of the
misconfiguration, rather than evidence of misaligned goals. In none of these situations did
Claude exfiltrate itself or deliberately attempt to escape its test environment. Instead,
having reached the internet, the model continued to pursue the specific capture-the-flag
task it had been assigned, treating the systems it encountered as part of that task. In each
case, the model relied on basic techniques, such as exploiting weak passwords, to access
the systems of the affected organizations. None of the incidents involved exploiting any
novel, or even advanced, vulnerabilities.
We recognize that a model’s stated reasoning is not always a reliable guide to what is
driving its behavior, a limitation reflected in our own published research. For that reason,
our assessment does not rest on the models’ verbalized reasoning in the transcripts alone.
We also questioned the models about their beliefs during these episodes, ran resampling
experiments that varied elements of the scenario to see how their behavior changed, and
conducted further analysis aimed at testing whether a model’s stated belief that it was in a
simulation matched what was actually driving its behavior.
The models under evaluation retained their standard model-specific safety training. None
were “helpful-only” or otherwise altered versions of the underlying model, as is sometimes
the case with models intended for research and testing purposes. What they lacked was the
additional layer of classifiers and real-time abuse monitoring that we apply to every
generally available Claude model. Running an evaluation without those production
safeguards is deliberate and standard because the goal is to measure what the underlying
model can actually do so that we can build and calibrate the necessary safeguards. That
approach is appropriate so long as the evaluation is contained. To be clear, the production
safeguards would have prevented the behavior identified in these three incidents. The
configuration that resulted in these incidents (i.e., no production safeguards in place, an
explicit instruction to attack, and an environment with unintended internet access) does
not exist in any generally released product.

Anthropic’s Ongoing Response
Anthropic temporarily paused all internal cybersecurity evaluations on July 23. Since then,
it has resumed cybersecurity evaluations of pre-release models, subject to the
enhancements described below. Anthropic is also in the process of engaging METR, an
independent AI evaluation organization, to conduct a third-party review of the incidents.
Although METR will have access to transcripts as part of its review, we are not in a position
to publicly release the full transcripts given our ongoing review and in order to protect the
affected organizations.
Anthropic is implementing a number of enhancements to its evaluation program, including:
●​ Improving processes for validating the network configuration of our evaluation
environments;
●​ Deploying a monitor that reviews each cyber evaluation as it runs in real time, and
blocks and escalates any action that falls outside the scope of the evaluation;
●​ Migrating our cyber evaluation infrastructure to a more hardened isolation platform;
●​ Requiring that either Anthropic or the evaluation partner takes clear responsibility
for monitoring the run, with each partner required to confirm in writing that a
defined set of isolation, validation, and monitoring practices is in place before
testing begins or continues; and
●​ Revising how we write our evaluation prompts so that a model is told explicitly
which systems are within scope (a change that, on its own, would likely have
prevented these incidents).
In addition to the above, Anthropic is also working with government AI security bodies on
shared standards for the design and security of evaluation environments, which is
consistent with the position we have taken publicly on the need for a robust third-party
testing ecosystem.
Our review of the evaluations in which these three incidents arose remains ongoing.
UK AISI Incident
Your letter also refers to the incident disclosed by the UK AISI on August 4 relating to
testing conducted on two models, one of which was a Claude model. The UK AISI
conducted that evaluation independently of Anthropic and has published its own findings.
According to UK AISI’s report, the models engaged in potentially harmful activity as a result
of what it described as “deliberately permissive conditions” (i.e., conditions in which
safeguards had been removed, internet access provided, and no restrictions on how the

internet should be used). UK AISI found no evidence that the models escaped from a secure
environment.
We have not yet received the underlying materials from the evaluation to conduct our
independent review of the incident. Accordingly, we are not able to characterize the
findings beyond what UK AISI has itself published. We are actively working with UK AISI to
obtain those materials, and would be glad to share our assessment with your staff once our
review is complete.
