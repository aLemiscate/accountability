OpenAI’s Harsh NDA Prompts Sam Altman to Say Sorry
When key staff members left OpenAI, apparently worried about the company’s grip on safety, it triggered concerns. The attention revealed the prohibitions on what exactly the company lets former employees say.

OpenAI’s CEO Sam Altman is no stranger to the spotlight, whether he’s courting it by making bold statements about the risks of the technology or being thrust into it in the fallout from last year’s dramatic leadership team shuffle.
So it’s no surprise Altman is in the hot seat again. This time the kerfuffle is about the way OpenAI handles ex-employees–or rather how the company tries to dictate what they cannot say about their old employer, whose nondisclosure agreement language stands out for the harshness of its non-disparagement clause. Employees who leave, as two senior figures did last week, are banned for life from speaking ill of OpenAI via an NDA that threatens to take away their stock should they let slip an unkind utterance–or even acknowledge that they’re bound by such an agreement.

The focus on what would normally be dreary, standard office documentation comes after the departure of OpenAI’s former chief scientist Ilya Sutskever and safety team member Jan Leike. As Vox.com points out, Sutskever’s departure wasn’t entirely unexpected, even though its timing near a big company event was interesting. He was involved in the boardroom revolt that briefly saw Altman ejected from his role, and never returned to the office. But Leike, who ran the so-called superalignment team at the company–created to make sure AIs develop safely in line with humanity’s needs and goals–was a surprise departure, and his blunt “I resigned” X posting on May 15 stirred up speculation.
What worrying aspects of OpenAI had these two guys seen, and what were they not saying? Even when Leike returned to X a few days later, to explain more about his departure, his careful statements on worries that “safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products” were relatively mild and didn’t blame anyone in particular.
Sutskever’s relative silence about his former employer since last year’s drama and Leike’s post, which does somewhat disparage the company but artfully sidesteps specifics, make more sense when you realize both were likely thinking of OpenAI’s NDA and were concerned about putting their stock at risk.
Vox’s Kelsey Piper reviewed a copy of the NDA, and noted it contained an “extremely restrictive off-boarding agreement” that includes both “nondisclosure and non-disparagement provisions.” In essence, by taking a contract at OpenAI, workers are forbidden “for the rest of their lives” from criticizing OpenAI once they’ve left. To add an extra Orwellian angle, “even acknowledging that the NDA exists is a violation of it.”
Violation of an NDA may, in some cases with other companies, result in legal action. But in this NDA, the terms also specified that violation could mean any vested equity the ex-employee had in OpenAI would be withdrawn. Part of the perks for working in high-stakes, high-stress startups like OpenAI is that your early employee stock could one day make you rich.
On Saturday, after Leike’s X posts garnered attention, Altman himself spoke up on X, and it seemed like he was doing the right thing about the NDA. OpenAI had “never clawed back anyone’s vested equity,” Altman explained, nor would it do so “if people do not sign a separation agreement (or don’t agree to a non-disparagement agreement).” As far as Altman is concerned, “vested equity is vested equity, full stop.”
Altman even took the next step and made it personal: The clawback provision “should never have been something we had in any documents or communication” he said, adding “this is on me and one of the few times i’ve been genuinely embarrassed running openai.” His next words are a bit more troublesome: “i did not know this was happening and i should have.” Oops. At least Altman said the company is fixing its “standard exit paperwork” and he offered that any former employee worried about things could contact him. “Very sorry about this,” he ended.
Conclude what you wish from Altman’s apology. It’s commendable for its speediness, and his ownership of the problem, even though he dodges and says it wasn’t his fault in the first place. It does at least directly address an issue that could quickly become a legally expensive drama, and it contains the word “sorry.”
The fuss is also a reminder that if your company uses NDAs, you need to be aware they’re controversial at best and you may not need them in the first place. Non-disparagement clauses in particular are particularly thorny, legally speaking, as the recent legal tangle involving former employees at Elon Musk’s SpaceX shows.
